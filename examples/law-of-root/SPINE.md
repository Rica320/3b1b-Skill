# The Law of Root — narrative spine

## Spine in one paragraph

Root gives every player a different rulebook, which sounds like it should stop
being one game. The obvious thing that holds it together — a shared 30-point
scoreboard — turns out not to be enough: four factions with four private
scoring engines could each grow in their own corner and never meet. What
actually binds them is the ground. Every faction's engine has to be plugged
into the same map, and on that map there is exactly one sentence that decides
everything: *the ruler of a clearing is the player with the most warriors and
buildings there.* Move needs it. Building needs it. Crafting is paid in
clearings. And then the reveal: every faction in the box is an **edit to that
one sentence** — the Eyrie rule on ties, the Cult rules wherever it has a
garden, the Vagabond can never rule at all, the Alliance scores with tokens
that don't count toward it, the Riverfolk swim past it. Six factions, six
answers to the same question. That is what makes it one game.

## Driving question

> **"No two players in Root are playing by the same rules. So what makes it one
> game?"**

Lands at **0:18**, over the board. Restated verbatim at the payoff (~7:50).

## Carried metaphor — THE SKYLINE

One object: the Woodland map, seen as terrain with things standing on it.
Introduced at 1:30, on screen at the end. It is *literally true* — every tower
height in the render is `warriors + buildings` computed from a real game state,
not hand-keyed.

| Concept (rule) | Same object, later |
|---|---|
| a clearing (2.2) | one disc of ground, with slots |
| presence | a tower of blocks standing in it |
| **rule (2.8)** | the tallest tower — and ties mean nobody |
| tokens/pawns don't count toward rule (2.8) | they lie **flat** on the ground, zero height |

## The 3D justification (Rule 9)

The scene opens **top-down (phi = 0)** — a board, exactly as it sits on a table.
The question "who rules this clearing?" cannot be *seen* from there; the pieces
are a scatter and you have to count them. The camera then tilts to **phi ≈ 64**
and the pieces stand up: rule becomes a skyline you read at a glance. Nothing
else on stage changes during that move. That is the only reason this video is
3D, and it is spent once, at 2:05. It tilts back to flat for the final frame.

## Sections

### §0 — The question  (~0:50)
**Narration.** Root looks like a board game about woodland animals. It is a war
game, and it has a strange property: no two players are playing by the same
rules. The cat scores by building. The birds score by nesting. The mouse
alliance scores by making people sympathetic to her cause. Different pieces,
different actions, different ways to win. So what makes it one game?

**Visuals.** The real board photo, held for a beat, then dissolving into the
clean Woodland graph beneath it (the twelve clearing discs animate into place
out of the photo). Six faction crests come up in a row, each in its colour.
The question is written and held over a still, dark map. Wordless beat.

**Why this follows.** Nothing yet — this is the hole the rest fills.

### §1 — The obvious answer, and how it fails  (~0:45)
**Narration.** The obvious answer is the scoreboard. Every faction scores
differently, but they all score onto the same track, and the first player to
thirty points wins immediately. One finish line. Except a shared finish line is
not a shared game. If the cat can build in her corner, and the birds can nest
in theirs, and neither ever has to look at the other, then this is a race —
four players playing solitaire at the same table. Something has to force them
into the same room.

**Visuals.** The score track appears along the bottom, four markers on zero.
Two factions grow in opposite corners, towers rising, both markers climbing,
never touching — the failure, shown rather than asserted. Then both are dimmed
and the question of §2 is posed on the empty middle of the map.

**Why this follows.** §0 asked what makes it one game; the first answer anyone
gives is "they share a scoreboard", and it is fair, and it is not enough.

### §2 — The ground, and one sentence  (~1:25)
**Narration.** Here is the Woodland. Clearings, joined by paths — a clearing is
adjacent to every clearing a path links it to. Every clearing has a suit: fox,
rabbit, or mouse. Every clearing has slots, and a building fills one; four of
those slots start the game filled with ruins. Rivers run through it, and rivers
are paths for exactly one faction. That is the board. Now the sentence that the
whole game hangs on. *The ruler of a clearing is the player with the most total
warriors and buildings in it.* Tokens don't count. Pawns don't count. And if two
players tie, nobody rules. From up here you can't see that. You have to count.
So stop looking at it like a map.

**Visuals.** Ground discs, paths, suit legend, slots, ruins, the river ribbon.
Pieces are placed flat, top-down — deliberately hard to read. Rule 2.8 written
out. **THE TILT** (phi 0 → 64, 3.6s, nothing else moving) and the pieces stand
up into towers. Wordless beat on the skyline.

**Why this follows.** §1 ended needing something that forces contact. The map is
that thing, and the sentence about rule is where the forcing actually lives.

### §3 — Rule is a permission slip  (~0:50)
**Narration.** Ruling a clearing is not a prize. It is a permission slip. To
move, you take warriors from one clearing to one adjacent clearing — and you
must rule the origin, or the destination, or both. To build, you need a clearing
you rule and an open slot. And crafting is paid in clearings: a card's cost is
paid by activating your pieces, and a piece's suit is the suit of the clearing
it stands in. So whatever private machine your faction runs on, it is bolted to
this board. There is no corner to be left alone in.

**Visuals.** One move animated with the rule test shown as a height comparison
(the origin tower overtops, so the move is legal; then a tie, and it isn't).
A build filling a slot. A craft cost paid by lighting up two clearings of the
right suits.

**Why this follows.** §2 gave the sentence; this is why the sentence has teeth.

### §4 — The turn, and the one action that changes the skyline  (~1:20)
**Narration.** A turn has three phases, always in this order. Birdsong, before
the day — the setup phase. Daylight — where most actions happen. Evening —
where you draw cards, and where some factions score. Then the next player, going
clockwise. Most of what you do in Daylight is quiet: draw, craft, move. Battle
is the loud one. You choose a clearing where you have warriors, you choose
someone else there to defend, and you roll both dice. The attacker deals hits
equal to the higher roll. The defender deals hits equal to the lower one. But
you can never deal more hits from the dice than you have warriors standing in
that clearing — so the roll is a ceiling, and your tower is the real limit. If
the defender has no warriors there at all, the attacker deals one extra hit.
Then both sides deal their hits at the same time, warriors first, and buildings
and tokens only after every warrior is gone. And every enemy building or token
you remove is worth a victory point.

**Visuals.** The three phases as a HUD band. A battle in one clearing, from
above at first, then at skyline height: two towers, two dice, hits taken as
blocks knocked off, the cap shown as a line at tower height, simultaneity shown
by both towers losing blocks on the same frame. The ruler flips. Wordless beat.

**Why this follows.** §3 said every engine is bolted to the board; battle is
how the bolts get contested.

### §5 — Six factions, six edits to one sentence  (~1:45)
**Narration.** Now the part that looks like chaos. Six factions, six rulebooks —
and every one of them is written against that same sentence. The Marquise de
Cat plays it straight, and hardest: she scores by building, and buildings are
warriors' worth of height, so her score and her grip on the map are the same
thing. She needs wood to build it, and the wood comes from sawmills she already
built. The Eyrie Dynasties edit the sentence: they rule a clearing when they are
merely *tied* for most — but never an empty one. They score every Evening for
roosts standing on the map, so they grow faster than anyone. And they are bound
by the Decree: every Birdsong they add cards to it, and every Daylight they must
carry out every action on it. Miss one, and they fall into turmoil. The Woodland
Alliance ignores the sentence. She scores by placing sympathy — and sympathy is
a token, and tokens do not count toward rule. Her whole score is invisible to
the skyline. Better: move warriors into a clearing she has made sympathetic, and
Outrage makes you hand her a card. Attacking her funds her. The Vagabond deletes
the sentence. He is a pawn, not a warrior — he cannot rule a clearing, and he
cannot stop anyone else ruling one, and he cannot be removed from the map. He
moves regardless of who rules anything, and he scores by carrying items and
making friends. The Lizard Cult overwrites the sentence: wherever the Cult has a
garden, the Cult rules — even over the Eyrie. And the Riverfolk Company goes
around it: rivers are paths for them and nobody else, and they can move along one
no matter who rules either end. They sell services to the other five.

**Visuals.** Each faction: crest, colour, and one animated claim in the skyline
grammar — the cat's tower growing with a building block, the birds' tie shown as
two equal towers where blue still rules, the alliance's flat green tokens spreading
with no height, the vagabond as a grey pawn sitting beside a tower it cannot
touch, a cult garden turning a clearing yellow regardless of height, a teal
warrior swimming along the river past a clearing it doesn't rule.

**Why this follows.** §4 forced everyone onto the same ground; this is what each
of them does about it — and it is the answer to §0 forming.

### §6 — Three turns  (~2:00)
**Narration.** Watch it run. Setup: the Marquise takes a corner, puts her keep
in it, and garrisons a warrior in every clearing except the corner diagonally
opposite — which is where the Eyrie start, with a roost and six birds. The
Alliance starts with nothing on the board at all. Turn one, the Marquise.
Birdsong: a wood token at each sawmill. Daylight: three actions. She builds —
paying the wood, filling a slot, scoring. She marches, twice. Her towers get
taller. Evening: she draws. Turn two, the Eyrie. Birdsong: add a card to the
Decree. Daylight: resolve every column, in order — recruit, move, battle. The
birds move into a clearing the cat rules and attack. Higher roll to the
attacker, lower to the defender, capped by warriors present, dealt at the same
time. The tower falls, and the clearing changes hands. Evening: the birds score
for every roost on the map. Turn three, the Alliance. Birdsong: she spreads
sympathy — spending supporters that match the clearing, one extra if there are
three or more enemy warriors standing there. She scores, and she has still not
placed a single warrior. And now watch what the cat's obvious reply costs her.
The Marquise marches into the sympathetic clearing — and Outrage makes her hand
the Alliance a matching card. Which the Alliance spends, next Birdsong, on a
revolt: every enemy piece in that clearing is removed, and a base goes up in
their place.

**Visuals.** A real game state, driven by a rules model — every action checked
legal before it is animated. Score track live at the bottom. The camera stays at
skyline height and pushes in on the clearing that matters each turn.

**Why this follows.** Everything above, running at once.

### §7 — Payoff  (~0:40)
**Narration.** So: no two players in Root are playing by the same rules. What
makes it one game? They are not playing different games. They are playing
different answers to the same question — who holds this clearing. The cat
answers with buildings. The birds answer by tying. The alliance answers by not
needing to hold it. The vagabond answers that he never can. Six rulebooks, one
sentence, one map. The rules differ. The ground doesn't.

**Visuals.** The question returns verbatim, in the styling it had at 0:18. The
full map at skyline height, six colours standing on it. The camera tilts back to
flat — the board on the table — and the photo returns underneath.

## Verified against the rules

Every rule claim in the script is cited to a section of *The Law of Root* and
checked by `verify_root.py`, which reads the claim list and re-derives each one
from the rules model. No printed value that is not in the rules reference (VP
values on faction boards, building costs, service prices) is asserted.

## Open choices — as resolved

*(Answered before implementation; recorded here as the build's contract.)*

1. **The map.** The supplied board photo is 608×504 and shot at an angle;
   adjacency and suits cannot be read from it reliably. Default: a *structurally
   faithful* Woodland — 12 clearings, 4 fox / 4 rabbit / 4 mouse, 4 corner
   clearings, 4 ruins, a river — laid out for legibility. One table in
   `woodland.py` swaps in the exact autumn adjacency if you have it.
2. **Faction board numbers.** The rules reference has no VP values or building
   costs — those are printed on the faction boards. Without them the example
   shows the mechanism and says "score the points the track reveals".
3. **Runtime** lands around 8 minutes. This is a rules explainer; that is what
   clarity costs.

### Resolutions

1. **The map** — schematic Woodland, as described above. Swap `CLEARINGS` and
   `PATHS` in `woodland.py` for the printed autumn adjacency at any time.
2. **Faction board numbers** — still outstanding. The video currently says
   "the points the space underneath reveals" and moves the marker without
   asserting a figure, so nothing in it can be wrong. When the Marquise, Eyrie
   and Alliance boards arrive, the values go into `demo_game.py` and only the
   affected narration beats need re-cutting.
3. **Faction depth** — Marquise, Eyrie, Alliance and Vagabond in full; Lizard
   Cult and Riverfolk Company in one beat each.
